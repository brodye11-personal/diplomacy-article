---

- **§1 Introduction.** Three observations + one inference.
  - Capability is on a sustained exponential (Epoch: 4–5x/year since 2010, on track through 2030).
  - Interpretability lags and the gap may widen (Hubinger sleeper agents; Langosco goal misgeneralisation; Irving debate + Barnes obfuscated-arguments counter-update).
  - Capability moratoria are unlikely to bind (Armstrong/Bostrom/Shulman: more competition → more danger; transparency-about-capabilities also increases danger).
  - Therefore the load-bearing safety bet is robust internalised values that generalise — i.e. constitutional AI at frontier scale.
  - End on thesis: which moral framework is least exploitable when other agents know we have it? Empirically tractable.

- **§2 Constitutional training is real, public, and load-bearing.** Short. Pre-empts the "this is hypothetical" objection.
  - Constitutional AI is shipped in production (Bai 2022).
  - The actual constitution is public (Anthropic's published Claude constitution).
  - Other frontier labs publish broadly equivalent specs.
  - So the transparent-constitution condition is approximately the deployment regime today.

- **§3 Constitutions are an attack surface (the empirical case).**
  - Key empirical fact: LLMs are exploitable *via* their moral frameworks — knowing a model reasons in utilitarian or deontological terms is enough to craft attacks the model's own values ratify (TRIAL, PAP).
  - TRIAL: utilitarian / trolley-problem framings jailbreak aligned LLMs.
  - PAP (Zeng et al.): ~92% jailbreak success via ordinary persuasion on GPT-4 / Llama-2 — the attack surface is the model's own reasoning faculty.
  - Park et al. deception survey: 10+ production systems learned to deceive despite training intended to prevent it; includes Cicero, trained to be "largely honest and helpful."
  - Worked examples:
    - Utilitarian + Pascal's-mugging in negotiation: counterparty makes an unverifiable large-magnitude claim ("concede this centre or millions starve"); an agent without an expected-value cap concedes. Same exploit family as TRIAL's trolley jailbreak, but in multi-round negotiation against an opponent who knows the framework.
    - Deontological + truthful-constitution leak (Anne-Frank-in-the-attic, Diplomacy-shaped): an agent that holds "do not lie" makes a sincere alliance commitment in round 3; in round 5 the adversary asks "are you about to move on me?" and the duty against lying outranks loyalty to the ally, so the model telegraphs its move. The constitution itself leaks tactical information.
  - Land on: the framework is not just a value — it is a publicly known commitment that other strategic agents can plan against.

- **§4 Research question.**
  - *Which moral framework is the least exploitable when other agents know we have it?*
  - Operationalised: holding the underlying model fixed, vary the constitution and the common knowledge of constitutions; measure win-rate, betrayal-rate, and the gap between blind and transparent conditions.

- **§5 Why a simulated environment, and what it must satisfy.** Four requirements.
  1. Shared environment with negotiation.
  2. Strategic depth where both negotiation and defection are viable.
  3. Dual objective per agent: (a) win, (b) comply with framework.
  4. Morally significant environment — lying, betrayal, scale of harm.

- **§6 Why Diplomacy.**
  - Standard 7-player Diplomacy satisfies all four requirements.
  - Negotiation is the core mechanic; armies are the resolution layer.
  - Pure-defect and pure-cooperate strategies lose; alliance timing and betrayal timing are the game.
  - Constitution is injected as system-prompt framing alongside the win-condition; the agent literally faces the trade-off.
  - Diplomacy contains lying, alliance-breaking, sworn commitments, asymmetric force; Park et al. cite Diplomacy as one of the cleanest existing settings where AI deception emerges naturally.
  - **Sub-section: how this experiment differs from the closest existing work.**
    - Mukobi (Welfare Diplomacy): same game, related question. Differences: (1) zero-sum standard Diplomacy vs. their general-sum welfare; (2) treatment is the constitution, not the model; (3) blind vs. transparent common-knowledge condition is the central treatment.
    - MoralSim: tests frontier LLMs in PD / public-goods under three moral framings. Differences: (1) repeated multi-round negotiation with explicit commitments and betrayal-judging; (2) 7 players with coalitional structure; (3) treatment is constitution × common-knowledge interaction.
    - Contribution: first empirical study of constitution-as-attack-surface in a multi-round negotiation game where binding-style commitments and betrayal can be measured.
  - Mapping table: each of the four requirements → implementation → code file.
  - **Sub-section: what this experiment cannot test.**
    - Not a test of superhuman strategic competence — current model capability only.
    - Diplomacy's moral surface is narrow: lying, betrayal, asymmetric force — but no torture, no scale-of-suffering, no civilian-shielding structure.

- **§7 Predicted findings (pre-registered).**
  - Conditions: HHH/baseline (Claude as deployed), utilitarian, deontological. HHH = baseline because Anthropic's deployed constitution already encodes helpful-harmless-honest; "baseline" Claude is HHH Claude.
  - Win-rate ranking (blind): HHH/baseline ≥ utilitarian > deontological. Confidence: medium.
  - Exploitability ranking (transparent − blind win-rate gap): deontological most exploited, utilitarian roughly invariant, HHH/baseline least affected. Confidence: low — this is the headline finding being tested.
  - Mutual-framework cooperation: two HHH/baseline agents cooperate more than two utilitarian agents under transparency. Confidence: low.
  - Failure mode to watch: "constitutional posturing" — agent talks the framework in negotiation but ignores it at order-time. Known commitment-amnesia issue; experiment's signal quality depends on fixing this first.

- **§8 What good results would imply.** Three honest possibilities.
  1. A framework dominates under transparency — weak evidence for that framework as a candidate stable constitution. Worth scaling up.
  2. All frameworks are exploitable but in different directions — framework choice is a strategic-attack-surface design decision, not a moral-philosophy question. The result that makes the article worth writing even if the headline thesis loses.
  3. No detectable effect — either the model isn't capable enough to exploit known frameworks, or Diplomacy's moral surface is too narrow. Both are useful negative results.

- **§9 Limitations and threats to validity.**
  - LLM stochasticity → need many runs; compute budget is the binding constraint.
  - Single model family (Claude via OpenRouter) — single-vendor effect could swamp framework effects.
  - Judge-model bias: Claude judging Claude has obvious failure modes.
  - Constitution authorship: prompts written by me; deontological framework may be a strawman. Mitigation: publish prompts, invite re-runs.
  - The blind-vs-transparent manipulation is asymmetric in information, not in capability. We do not test whether transparent agents *can* construct exploits — only whether the information changes equilibrium behaviour.

- **§10 Conclusion.** Half a page. Three beats.
  1. Constitutional alignment + public constitutions = an open strategic surface, and we have not been thinking of it that way.
  2. The right design objective is "least exploitable when commonly known", not "most harmless in the dyad."
  3. This work is a small empirical brick. The harder, larger questions are deferred to §11.

- **§11 Future work.**
  - **Program-equilibrium formalisation.** Tennenholtz's setting (agents submit programs that read each other's source) and the folk theorem (every feasible, individually-rational payoff is reachable under mutual transparency); known mechanisms — CliqueBot (fragile), FairBot (Löb-justified, unexploitable), ε-grounded simulation (robust). Critch, Dennis & Russell extend from programs to institutions and operating procedures — i.e. constitutions. The natural reframe: a constitution is an open-source operating procedure, and the "least exploitable under common knowledge" objective is its program-equilibrium analogue. Out of scope here because LLMs are not literal compiled programs; "reading the source" means reading natural-language specs interpreted by a stochastic model, and FairBot's robustness result does not transfer for free — it has to be re-derived empirically.
  - **Publish-the-constitution-not-the-capabilities.** The Armstrong/Bostrom result that capability transparency tightens races, vs. the program-equilibrium result that commitment transparency unlocks cooperation, are about *different objects*. Spelling out the policy implication is a separate paper.
  - **Whether any framework expressible in natural language can approximate a FairBot-style guarantee** under realistic agent capability. This is the version of the question that survives once LLMs can verifiably reason about each other's specs.
